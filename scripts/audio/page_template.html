The short answer

Opus, unless something else has to play it. It wins every published listening test it has entered, at every bitrate tested, and it is royalty free. The one real catch is hardware: car head units, older Bluetooth stacks, hi-fi streamers and set-top boxes very often speak AAC and MP3 and not Opus. That is a distribution problem, not a quality one, and it is the only reason AAC is still the right default for anything you hand to a stranger.

What this machine can honestly measure

Everything below was produced by the scripts beside this page, on a corpus of . None of it is a quality score. There is no PEAQ, no ViSQOL and no listening panel here, and turning a signal difference into a quality ranking would be the single most misleading thing this page could do. What follows are properties that are true by construction: what the encoder emitted, how fast, how large, and which part of the spectrum it kept.

Effective audio bandwidth: where each codec stops coding

Measured on the stationary full band probe, where the question has an unambiguous answer.

Bandwidth is not quality. It is one measurable property and it explains one specific artefact, the dull muffled sound of a low bitrate MP3. It says nothing about how well the bands that survive are coded. Opus keeps the top octave at bitrates where MP3 throws it away, but Opus is also filling that octave with coarsely coded noise energy rather than detail. Read this chart as "what the codec chose to keep", not as a ranking.

The constraint that actually decides

Who can play it

Compression efficiency has a clear ranking and it barely moves. Reach is where the real decision lives, and for audio it is worse than for images, because the awkward decoders are not browsers, they are car stereos, Bluetooth chips, DAB radios and set-top boxes that were built once and never updated.

"Transcoded" in the Bluetooth column is the honest answer for almost everything: A2DP carries SBC, AAC, aptX or LDAC, so whatever format is on disk is decoded and re-encoded before it reaches the headphones. There is no Opus profile in A2DP, which is why Opus over Bluetooth is always a second lossy generation; Bluetooth LE Audio replaced SBC with LC3, which is not Opus either. Two more corrections the table earns: classic DAB carries MPEG-1 Layer II rather than MP3, and DAB+ carries HE-AAC v2, whose core layer is AAC-LC. And Safari was the last browser to the Opus party, only decoding Opus in Ogg and WebM from version 18.4 in March 2025, which is why the iOS cell is marked rather than green.

Operational

What to actually type

Bitrates in the comments are what these settings measured on this corpus, not encoder documentation.

Music, your own pipeline, best result per byte

# Opus. VBR is the default and it is the right default.
opusenc --bitrate 128 --vbr music.wav music.opus   # measured 
opusenc --bitrate 96  --vbr music.wav music.opus   # measured 
ffmpeg -i music.wav -c:a libopus -b:a 128k -vbr on music.opus

# above roughly 160 kbps stereo Opus is already past the point
# where any listening test could tell it from the source

Music, handed to someone else's device

# AAC. Use a good encoder: Apple's qaac or fdk-aac, not ffmpeg's native one.
qaac --tvbr 91 music.wav -o music.m4a       # true VBR, roughly 192 kbps
fdkaac -m 4 music.wav -o music.m4a          # the free alternative
ffmpeg -i music.wav -c:a aac -b:a 192k music.m4a  # the pessimistic case, see below

# MP3 when it truly has to be an MP3
lame -V2 music.wav music.mp3   # measured , transparent for most listeners
lame -V0 music.wav music.mp3   # measured , the archival end of MP3
lame -V5 music.wav music.mp3   # measured , the 2014 test setting

Speech, podcasts, voice notes

# Opus, mono, and let it drop into its speech coder
opusenc --bitrate 32 --vbr --downmix-mono talk.wav talk.opus
ffmpeg -i talk.wav -c:a libopus -b:a 32k -ac 1 -application voip talk.opus

# if the podcast host or the feed demands MP3, go mono first
lame --abr 64 -m m talk.wav talk.mp3

# what not to do: 64 kbps stereo MP3. Half the bitrate is spent
# on a stereo image a single voice does not have.

Archiving and masters

# FLAC, verified bit exact. -8 is the sensible top of the ladder.
flac -8 --verify master.wav                 #  off WAV on this corpus
ffmpeg -i master.wav -c:a flac -compression_level 8 master.flac

# -12 exists and buys almost nothing for a lot of time
#   level 8  
#   level 12 

# ALAC only if the Apple ecosystem forces it
ffmpeg -i master.wav -c:a alac master.m4a   #  off WAV, larger than FLAC

Method

How these numbers were produced, and what is missing

No perceptual metric exists here

PEAQ, ViSQOL and POLQA are all absent, and no listening panel was run. That is the reason for the two graph structure. Every quality claim on this page is cited to a published test and marked as such, and nothing in graph B is presented as a quality score.

The AAC numbers are the pessimistic case

Encoding went through ffmpeg's native AAC encoder, which is the weakest of the widely used ones. The 2011 AAC test measured 0.69 of a grade between the best and worst encoders of the same format at the same bitrate, and ffmpeg's native encoder was not even in that field. Exactly the same caveat as stock libjpeg-turbo on the images page.

HE-AAC could not be measured at all

libfdk-aac is not present, so there is no HE-AAC v1 or v2 encoder in this environment. Everything on this page about HE-AAC comes from the cited tests. Its position on the map is a literature position, not a measurement, and it is marked that way in its cell.

WMA was not measured either

ffmpeg ships wmav1 and wmav2 encoders but not WMA Pro, and the two it has are not representative of what Microsoft shipped. Measuring them would have produced a number that looked precise and meant nothing. The 2005 and 2007 tests, where WMA Pro was genuinely competitive, are cited instead.

The corpus is small and short

Six real clips totalling under four minutes. It is enough for bandwidth, bitrate and speed, which are stable properties, and nowhere near enough for a quality judgement, which is one more reason quality is not attempted here.

Speed depends on this machine

Single threaded, one shared container, one CPU, process startup included in the timing. Read the ratios between codecs, not the absolute multiples, and expect any real machine to be faster.

The listening tests are old

The newest public multiformat test is from 2014 and the oldest cited here is from 2005. Every encoder in them has been improved since, Opus most of all. The direction of the results has held up in every later private test, but the exact margins are historical.

xHE-AAC is absent

The newest member of the AAC family, used by Netflix and for adaptive streaming, has no encoder here and no public multiformat listening test to cite. It is genuinely interesting at very low bitrate and this page has nothing honest to say about it.

Scripts and raw measurements live in the repository beside this page, so every figure can be regenerated or argued with. The listening-test numbers are transcribed from the public results pages linked above and are marked throughout as cited rather than measured.
