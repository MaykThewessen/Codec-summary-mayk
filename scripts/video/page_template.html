Normalise the bitrate to bits per pixel per frame and the question becomes answerable. AV1 came first at 540p, 720p and 1080p, at every quality target measured. What moves is how much it wins by: the lead grows with resolution, and grows again as bits get scarce. That is exactly the corner 4K streaming sits in.

How to normalise, which was the real question

Bits per pixel per frame

Megabits per second is not a property of a picture, it is a property of a picture and a resolution and a frame rate. Divide it out and you get one number that means the same thing everywhere: bits / (width × height × frames). Everything on this page uses it, which is why three resolutions can share one axis.

What familiar bitrates look like once normalised

Sorted from the most constrained to the most generous.

Read the 4K row against the 1080p rows and the hypothesis answers itself. A 4K60 stream at 15 Mbit/s is more compressed per pixel than a 1080p30 stream at 3 Mbit/s, not less. That is why the question "which codec wins at 4K" is largely "which codec wins when bits are scarce". 4K earns the modern codecs their keep twice over: they are furthest ahead when bits are scarce, and their lead also grows with resolution, as the chart two sections down shows.

libaom is not AV1's speed

libaom is the reference encoder, and it is slow on purpose. SVT-AV1, which is what production pipelines actually run, is roughly an order of magnitude faster at a similar quality and is not available in this environment. Read the AV1 bar as an upper bound on cost, not as a fact about the format.

Decode is the other half, and it is cheap

Decode cost per frame is a small fraction of encode cost for every codec here, and on any device with a hardware decoder it is essentially free. That asymmetry is the single most misunderstood thing about AV1.

Presets move this a lot

Everything here uses one preset per codec. Slower presets buy several percent of BD-rate for a multiple of the time; faster ones give it back. The choice is a business decision about how many times a file will be watched.

The constraint that actually decides

Who can play it

The ordering of the codecs barely moves, even if the size of the gaps does. Reach is where the real decision lives, and for video it has four independent parts: browsers, phone silicon, TV silicon, and whether a lawyer has to be involved.

Codec Browsers Phone HW decode TV / box HW decode Desktop GPU Licensing Typical use
H.264 / AVC all all all all pool, expiring The fallback rung of every ladder; WebRTC; anything that must just work
HEVC / H.265 Safari, Edge all all all 3 pools + holdouts Broadcast, Blu-ray UHD, Apple ecosystem, camera originals
VP9 all 2016+ most 2015+ royalty-free YouTube's workhorse; the safe non-H.264 rung on the open web
AV1 all 2023+ flagships most 2023+ RTX 30 / RDNA2 / Arc+ royalty-free YouTube and Netflix at scale; the top rung of a modern ladder
VVC / H.266 none almost none a few 2024+ none pool, unsettled Broadcast trials (DVB, ATSC 3.0). Not a delivery option on the web

The licensing column is the one that predicts adoption. H.264's pool was a single, predictable licence and it went everywhere. HEVC fractured into three pools plus unlicensed holders, and Chrome and Firefox never shipped it. AV1 exists because a consortium of the companies paying those bills decided to route around the problem. VVC has repeated HEVC's structure, and the browsers have again declined.

Cited, not measured

VVC / H.266, and why it is not on the curves

Nothing in this section was measured. No VVC encoder is available in this environment, so there is no VVC point on any chart and no VVC number derived from these clips. Everything below is quoted from published work, with the source named. Treat the figures as a different kind of evidence from the rest of the page.

What it is

VVC is Versatile Video Coding, standardised as ITU-T H.266 in July 2020 by the same joint group that produced H.264 and HEVC. The name is the answer to the "VVC1" question: there is no VVC1 and no VVC2, just VVC, also called H.266.

How much better, per the literature

Nguyen and Marpe (APSIPA Transactions, 2021) measured average bitrate savings of 36 to 37% for the VVC reference encoder against the HEVC reference encoder under JVET common test conditions, and 10 to 15% for AV1 against the same baseline. Subjective testing in the standard's own overview paper reports up to 50% against HEVC. Bitmovin's independent run landed near 45%.

Why nobody ships it

Not compute. VVC decode is heavier than HEVC but well within a modern SoC, and software decoders exist. The blocker is licensing: VVC arrived with a patent pool structure that looks like HEVC's, the one that kept HEVC out of Chrome and Firefox for a decade. Browsers have not shipped VVC and have given no indication they intend to.

What to do about it

Nothing, for web delivery. If you are in broadcast, DVB and ATSC 3.0 profiles exist and the calculation is different. For everyone else the practical successor to HEVC is AV1, and the successor to AV1 will be AV2, for the same royalty-free reason.

Sources: T. Nguyen and D. Marpe, "Compression efficiency analysis of AV1, VVC, and HEVC for random access applications", APSIPA Transactions on Signal and Information Processing, vol. 10, 2021. B. Bross et al., "Overview of the Versatile Video Coding (VVC) Standard and its Applications", IEEE TCSVT, 2021. Bitmovin, "State of Compression: Testing H.266/VVC vs H.265/HEVC". Corrections and confirmations

Received wisdom, checked against the measurements

Optimistic
"H.265 is like 50% better than H.264."

Half right
"YouTube streams VP9, which achieves maybe similar performance. One wins at low bitrate, the other at high. They are very equal."

Backwards
"AV1 needs a hardware decoder because it is a high computing load."

True, and it is the name that trips people up
"VVC1 is a very advanced video codec."

It is Versatile Video Coding, H.266, and there is no "1" in it. Technically the claim holds: the published figures put it well ahead of everything measured here. Practically it is unusable on the open web, and this page could not measure it at all. The advanced part is real; the availability is not. See the section above.

It never shipped
"I do not know if we have VP10."

You do not. VP10 was Google's in-progress successor to VP9 and it was never released. In 2015 Google folded that work into the Alliance for Open Media, and it became AV1 together with contributions from Cisco's Thor and Mozilla's Daala. So AV1 is VP10, under a name that reflects who owns it. There will not be a VP11 either; the next one is AV2.

Right question, wrong axis
"Maybe HEVC wins for 1080p between 1 and 10 Mbit/s, but something else wins at 4K."

The other half of the question

MP4, MOV, MKV, WebM

Containers do not compress anything. They decide what can go inside, how a player finds a seek point, and what survives a round trip. Four of them matter and they differ less than the arguments suggest.

Container Base Video it carries Streamable Subtitles Where it belongs
MP4 ISO BMFF H.264, HEVC, AV1, VP9 fMP4 / DASH / HLS TTML, WebVTT Delivery. The default, and the only one that is safe everywhere
MOV QuickTime + ProRes, DNx, timecode no QT text tracks Production. MP4 is a descendant of it; ProRes and edit metadata are the reason to keep it
MKV Matroska anything at all not over HTTP SRT, ASS, PGS, many Archive and local playback. Multiple tracks, chapters, real subtitle formats
WebM Matroska subset VP8, VP9, AV1 only yes WebVTT The royalty-free web lane. A deliberately narrowed MKV

The practical advice

Deliver MP4 unless you have a specific reason not to; it is the only container every player, browser and TV accepts. Archive MKV, because it will hold anything and it keeps every audio track, chapter and subtitle you had. Keep MOV only where ProRes or an NLE requires it.

Streaming is a muxing question

A plain MP4 puts its index at the end, so a player has to fetch the tail before it can start. -movflags +faststart moves it to the front, and fragmented MP4 splits the file into independently fetchable pieces, which is what DASH and HLS actually ship. MKV has no equivalent story over HTTP, which is the real reason it is not a delivery format.

Remuxing is free, transcoding is not

Changing container is a copy, not a re-encode: ffmpeg -i in.mkv -c copy out.mp4 takes seconds and loses nothing, as long as the target container can carry the codecs inside. If it cannot (ProRes into WebM, PGS subtitles into MP4) that is a container limit, not a quality one.

What to actually type

A delivery ladder that covers everyone

# top rung: smallest file, royalty-free, needs a modern decoder
ffmpeg -i in.mov -c:v libaom-av1 -b:v 0 -crf  -cpu-used 6 -g 48 out_av1.mp4
# production runs SVT-AV1 instead: roughly 10x faster for a similar result.
# its -crf scale is not libaom's, so re-measure rather than copying this number.

# middle rung: every browser, hardware on most devices, no licence
ffmpeg -i in.mov -c:v libvpx-vp9 -b:v 0 -crf  -cpu-used 2 -row-mt 1 out_vp9.webm

# bottom rung: it plays on everything ever made
ffmpeg -i in.mov -c:v libx264 -preset medium -crf  \
       -profile:v high -movflags +faststart out_h264.mp4

What could not be measured at all

No VVC/H.266 encoder, so every VVC figure on this page is cited. No SVT-AV1 or rav1e, so AV1 is represented by the slow reference encoder. No 4K source, because the derf collection tops out at 1080p and upscaling would have invented detail that was never there: the 4K conclusions come from the normalised axis, not from 4K encodes. No hardware encoders, so nothing here says anything about NVENC or QSV quality.
